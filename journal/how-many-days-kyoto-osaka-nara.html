How Many Days Do You Need for Kyoto, Osaka and Nara?

A few thoughts on staying long enough for a journey to begin feeling familiar.

People sometimes ask me, “How many days do I need for Kyoto, Osaka and Nara?”

Three days can be enough to make the journey. One day for Kyoto, one for Osaka, and one for Nara.

But if you ask what I would recommend, I would say five days or more.

Not because you need to add more famous places. I simply think a journey becomes more enjoyable when you have time to wake to the same morning more than once.

The Pleasure of Repeating a Day

Most itineraries encourage us to keep moving. A new district today, another city tomorrow, and a different hotel after that.

There is nothing wrong with travelling that way. When I was younger, I travelled through many countries myself.

Still, some of my fondest memories come from doing almost the opposite: leaving the same place each morning, walking the same road, and coming back again in the evening.

Three Mornings at Wat Pho

Many years ago, when I was travelling in Bangkok, I went to Wat Pho three days in a row.

It was not because I needed to see the reclining Buddha again and again.

What I liked was the journey there.

Each morning I left the same accommodation, followed the same streets, walked through the same market, and continued toward Wat Pho. The food-stall owner I had noticed yesterday was there again today. The market that had seemed unfamiliar on the first morning began to feel a little more recognisable on the second.

Nothing dramatic happened. I simply felt myself settling, very gently, into the everyday rhythm of that part of Bangkok.

The Joy of Doing the Same Thing Again

By the third morning, the pleasure was no longer in discovering something new. It was in recognising what I had already seen: the same turn in the road, the same market, and the same people beginning another ordinary day.

That was when I first understood one of the pleasures that only a longer stay can offer. Repetition does not always make a journey smaller. Sometimes it lets you enter more deeply into a place.

One Home, Many Different Days

This is why, in Kyoto and Osaka, I often recommend keeping one home base instead of changing hotels every day.

Each morning, you leave through the same entrance. You follow the same road to the station. Perhaps you stop at the same convenience store. In the evening, you return by the same route and open the same front door.

After a few days, the neighbourhood no longer feels like scenery between your accommodation and the train. It begins to feel like a small part of your own daily life.

Our companion article, Best Base for Exploring Kyoto and Osaka, explains the practical side of this kind of stay. For me, however, its quieter value is even more important.

Kyoto, Osaka, Nara—and Time to Return

One morning may take you to Kyoto. Go early, walk beneath the torii gates of Fushimi Inari, and let the old streets lead you slowly toward Kiyomizu-dera and Gion.

If you would like a gentle route for that day, I have written about how I would spend a day at Fushimi Inari, Kiyomizu-dera and Gion.

Another day may be for Osaka: bright signs, busy streets, and food eaten while it is still hot. The city offers a completely different kind of energy.

Then there is Nara, where temple paths, old trees, and deer invite a slower pace. Uji can fill another day with tea, the river, and time to sit.

The point is not to fit every possible destination into five days. It is to let each day feel different while the beginning and the ending remain reassuringly the same.

Why I Would Choose Five Days

Three days can be a good trip. Five days gives you something else.

By the third morning, you do not have to fill the day simply because it is available. You might return to the shrine you liked yesterday. You might visit the same café again. You might decide to do very little.

That is still travel.

Five days also gives you time to notice small changes: different light on the same street, another person behind the counter, the sound of the neighbourhood at a slightly later hour.

The Same House in the Evening

At The Showa House, I hope guests can experience this kind of rhythm.

Walk through the same entrance each morning. Follow the familiar road toward the station. Come back to the same tatami room at night.

Perhaps take a quiet morning walk around the neighbourhood. Perhaps learn what it feels like to sleep on a traditional Japanese futon and wake as the light reaches the shoji screens.

These are not grand attractions. But after several days, they may become part of how you remember Japan.

A Place to Return to Every Evening

Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, and Uji.

And one day with no particular plans.

I think all of them can become part of a memorable journey.

Walking the same road each day and returning to the same house each evening—that is a way of travelling I have always liked very much.

If The Showa House could become that small place to return to, nothing would make me happier.
