Why Stay in a Traditional Japanese House?

Famous sights show you Japan. Staying in an ordinary home lets you understand a little of how everyday life in Japan feels.

From Taka’s Notebook

I like traveling. When I visit another country, I naturally want to see the places that represent it. I like famous sights too.

But I am equally interested in ordinary local life. I like walking through residential streets, visiting local supermarkets, and going into the small shops that people in the neighbourhood use.

I wonder what people usually eat, where they shop, and what kind of homes they return to. This is still how I like to travel today.

I Like Hotels Too

I should say clearly that I also stay in hotels. They are convenient, comfortable, and very useful when you are tired.

But when you stay in a hotel, you may travel through a country without ever entering an ordinary local home. You sleep in a hotel bed, walk through hotel corridors, and wake up in a hotel room. That is one kind of travel.

When a guest from overseas enters an ordinary Japanese home, different questions can naturally appear. Is this what a Japanese entrance looks like? So this is where people take off their shoes. Is this how people live on tatami? Do shoji and fusuma divide rooms like this? Is this where a futon is laid out at night?

These things are ordinary to me. But for someone visiting Japan, they can show a little of how everyday life in a Japanese home works.

A House Is Different When You Live in It

Japan has many places where you can visit old houses. I enjoy visiting them too. You can walk through the building, look at the rooms, read the explanations, and take photographs. That can be very interesting.

But a house was originally made to be lived in, not simply observed. A house is different when you actually use it.

You return after sightseeing, take off your shoes at the entrance, put down your bag, and sit or stretch out in a tatami room. You might eat something from a local supermarket, take a bath, sleep on a futon, and wake up in the same house the next morning.

Nothing special has happened. That is exactly the point. There is a difference between looking at a Japanese house and using one as your home for a few days.

Something I Noticed After Starting The Showa House

After I started The Showa House, I noticed that there are many places where visitors can see older Japanese houses, but fewer places where they can actually stay in one.

Among the places where you can stay, many have been significantly remodeled when they became accommodation. That is completely natural. Renovation is often necessary for safety, and modern equipment can make an old building much more comfortable for guests.

I do not think a large renovation is wrong. But if what I want as a traveler is something close to an ordinary Japanese home, places that retain much of that everyday-home character seem surprisingly uncommon. An old building may remain while feeling quite different from the home it once was. That is simply something I began to notice.

The Showa House Was Already an Ordinary Home

The Showa House was not created as a tourist attraction. It was the ordinary small house where my great-aunt lived.

When I turned it into accommodation, I did not completely redesign it. I kept as much of the ordinary house as reasonably possible: the tatami rooms, shoji, fusuma, wooden fittings, patterned glass, and the basic feeling and proportions of the rooms.

The house is not untouched. It has been maintained and adapted for people traveling today. There is Wi-Fi, a thick mattress under each futon, and one tatami room stays a bedroom while another is used as a living room.

I wanted guests to be comfortable. I also did not want the house to become something completely different simply because it became a guesthouse.

Not a Museum, and Not an Exact Recreation of the Past

I do not want guests to feel that they are sleeping in a museum. The Showa House is a home, and it is meant to be used.

You do not need to wear kimono, eat Japanese food every day, follow a cultural program, or fold and store your futon unless you want to. You do not need to do anything special to experience the house.

Signs That This Was a Real Home

Some wooden fittings are worn, and there are small marks left by many years of use. I do not need to call every old mark “history” or “character.” It is simply what happens when a house has been used as a home for a long time.

Cleanliness and guest comfort still matter. But replacing every old surface just to make the house look new would also remove some of what makes this particular house itself.

You Do Not Need a Special Japanese Experience

After a full day in Kyoto or Osaka, you can return to Kuzuha, buy dinner or drinks near the station or at a local supermarket, and come back to the house. Take off your shoes, relax in the tatami room, take a bath, and sleep on a futon. That is enough.

Perhaps the ordinary moments are exactly where everyday life in another country becomes visible.

Moments That Are Not on the Itinerary

When I plan a trip, I think about what to see, where to eat, and which places need a reservation. Guests coming here may be deciding what to see in Kyoto, what to eat in Osaka, or whether to visit Nara.

But after a trip, people sometimes remember things that were never on the schedule: the feeling of a room in the morning, a local supermarket, a walk through a quiet neighbourhood, or finally sitting down after returning tired at the end of the day.

These moments cannot really be booked. They appear naturally after living somewhere for a few days.

Between Kyoto and Osaka, Then Returning Home

The Showa House is in Hirakata, between Kyoto and Osaka. From here, guests can visit Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, and Uji.

During the day, you explore Japan. In the evening, you return to an ordinary residential neighbourhood. The next morning, you leave from the same house again. After doing this for a few days, the accommodation may begin to feel less like a place to sleep and more like your temporary home in Japan.

Why Stay in a Traditional Japanese House?

For me, the reason is not simply that tatami, shoji, or an old house looks attractive. A house shows something about how people live.

You can visit Japanese architecture during the day. But when you stay in a home, you remove your shoes there, relax there, eat there, sleep there, and wake up there. The house becomes part of your routine for a few days.

You do not have to do anything special. Come home, spend an ordinary evening, and sleep. That may be the point.

Perhaps the reason to stay in a traditional Japanese house is not only to see Japanese architecture, but to use a Japanese home as your own home for a few days.
