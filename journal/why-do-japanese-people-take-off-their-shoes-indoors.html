Why Do Japanese People Take Off Their Shoes Indoors?

A small custom that marks the beginning of home

If you're visiting Japan for the first time, one of the first things you'll notice is that everyone takes off their shoes before entering a home.

You'll see it at family houses, traditional inns, temples, and sometimes even in schools or small businesses.

For Japanese people, it's such a normal habit that we rarely stop to think about it.

But why do we do it?

A Home Is Different from the Outside World

In Japan, the entrance to a home is more than just a doorway.

It marks the boundary between the outside world and a private living space.

By taking off your shoes, you leave behind the dust and dirt from outside and step into a cleaner, quieter environment.

It's a small action, but one that helps create a feeling of comfort and relaxation.

A Culture of Living Close to the Floor

Japanese homes have traditionally been designed for life on the floor.

People sit on tatami mats, children play on the floor, and many families sleep on futons laid directly on the tatami.

Because the floor is part of everyday living, keeping it clean has always been important.

Removing shoes is a simple way to care for the space where people spend most of their time.

More Than Just Keeping Things Clean

Of course, taking off your shoes helps keep the house clean.

But for many Japanese people, it also has another meaning.

It is a quiet moment that signals the end of being outside and the beginning of being at home.

For many visitors, this simple habit soon becomes surprisingly natural.

What Visitors Should Do

When entering a Japanese home, ryokan, temple room, or any space with tatami, look for a raised interior floor or a row of shoes near the entrance. This usually shows where outdoor footwear should come off.

Place your shoes neatly with the toes pointing toward the door, and avoid stepping on the entrance floor in your socks. If slippers are provided, remember that they are normally removed before stepping onto tatami.

The same custom applies at The Showa House. Rather than treating it only as a rule, it can help you notice how Japanese homes separate outdoor space from the clean, floor-level living area inside.

Sometimes, the Smallest Customs Tell the Biggest Stories

Many travelers come to Japan to see famous temples, castles, and gardens.

Those places are wonderful.

But sometimes, the moments that stay with us are much smaller.

Taking off your shoes, stepping onto tatami for the first time, and feeling at home in an unfamiliar place can become memories that last long after the journey ends.
