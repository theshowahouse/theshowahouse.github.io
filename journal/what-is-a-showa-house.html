What Is a Showa-Era Japanese House?

A guide to the ordinary Japanese homes whose materials, proportions, and everyday character have quietly become rare.

From Taka’s Notebook

I do not believe that everything was better in the past.

But after staying in this house many times, I began to notice qualities that are difficult to find in modern homes. Because it had not needed a major renovation for so long, a particular atmosphere had quietly remained here.

Then one day I remembered something about the way I travelled when I was younger. Wherever I went overseas, I always wanted to feel the everyday life of the people who lived there.

I enjoyed the famous sights, of course. But I also wanted to walk through residential streets, visit supermarkets, step into the small shops local people used, and understand something of an ordinary day in that place.

That has not changed as I have grown older.

And suddenly I thought, “This is exactly the kind of house I would want to stay in when I travel.”

That was where The Showa House began.

“Showa” refers to the Japanese era that lasted from 1926 to 1989. It was a long period of enormous change: Japan moved through war, recovery, rapid economic growth and the arrival of many things that shaped modern daily life.

A Showa house is not one fixed architectural style. The phrase is often used more loosely for homes that still carry the materials, proportions and atmosphere of everyday life from that era.

An ordinary home

For travellers, these homes can make familiar Japanese features easier to understand. Tatami rooms are flexible spaces rather than bedrooms with one fixed purpose, sliding doors divide rooms without heavy walls, and futon bedding can be stored away during the day.

The Showa House is one surviving example. It was the home of our great-aunt, who lived to be 100 years old, grew seasonal vegetables in the garden, and made kimono. Because she made few major changes, tatami rooms, wooden frames, patterned glass, and other signs of everyday Showa-era life remain.

What was once ordinary has quietly become something rare.

Why these homes feel different

Many older Japanese homes have been rebuilt as lifestyles, safety standards and ideas of comfort changed. That change is natural, but it also means that a particular kind of everyday atmosphere is disappearing.

For a visitor to Japan, the value is not luxury or historical grandeur. It is seeing how materials, light, storage, and floor-level living shaped an ordinary family home before modern layouts became standard.

A home, not a hotel

Staying in an older home also means accepting details that differ from a modern hotel. Traditional rooms may transmit more sound, futon replaces a raised bed, and shoes come off at the entrance. Understanding these customs makes the experience more comfortable and meaningful.

The Showa House offers this local experience in Hirakata while serving as a convenient base for Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, and Uji.
