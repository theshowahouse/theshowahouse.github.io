What Is a Showa-Era Japanese House?

A guide to the ordinary Japanese homes whose materials, proportions, and everyday character have quietly become rare.

From Taka’s Notebook

I enjoy seeing famous places when I travel. But I am just as interested in the ordinary lives of the people who live there.

I like walking through residential streets, visiting a local supermarket, and stepping into the small shops that people in the neighbourhood use.

I find myself wondering, “What do people in this town usually eat? Where do they shop? What kind of home do they return to?”

I have traveled this way since I was young. That part of me has not changed very much.

When I inherited this house, a thought came to me: “If I were traveling to Japan from overseas, would this be the kind of house I would want to stay in?”

It is not a famous cultural property or a luxurious ryokan. It is a small Japanese house where my great-aunt lived an ordinary life for many years.

But when I travel abroad, this kind of ordinary life is exactly what I want to see. I thought there might be visitors coming to Japan who feel the same way.

That is why I started The Showa House.

What Does “Showa-Era House” Mean?

“Showa” is the name of the Japanese era that lasted from 1926 to 1989. It was a long period, and life in Japan changed enormously during those years.

A Showa-era house is not one exact architectural style. I use the name more simply for a home that still carries the materials, proportions, and feeling of ordinary life from that time.

The Showa House was the home of my great-aunt. She lived to be 100 years old, grew seasonal vegetables in the garden, and made kimono. She was not preserving the house as a historical display. She was simply living here.

Because the house did not go through a major transformation, many familiar details remained: tatami rooms, wooden frames, patterned glass, sliding doors, and small places for everyday family objects.

I do not think everything was better in the past. But after spending time here, I began to notice qualities that are now difficult to find in many modern homes.

What was once ordinary has quietly become something rare.

The Small Details of an Ordinary Home

When I look around this house, I do not see grand architecture. I notice smaller things: the way light passes through patterned glass, the wooden frames around the rooms, the alcove where family objects were displayed, and the feeling of sitting close to the floor.

None of these things was unusual when the house was lived in. They were simply part of daily life.

Many older Japanese homes have been rebuilt as lifestyles, safety standards, and ideas of comfort have changed. That is natural. A house needs to work for the people who live in it.

At the same time, rebuilding means that some of this everyday atmosphere is disappearing. I began to feel that letting guests stay here could be a way to share it—not as an exhibit, but as a home.

A House Shaped by Everyday Life

The rooms also show how Japanese families used their homes. A tatami room did not always have one fixed purpose. People could sit together or eat around a low table during the day, then lay out futons and use the same room for sleeping at night.

Sliding doors could divide or connect rooms. Futons could be folded and stored away. Shoes came off at the entrance because the floor was also a place to sit, relax, and sometimes sleep.

These features were not created for tourists. They made sense for ordinary life in the house.

Staying here is naturally different from staying in a modern hotel. Sound carries more easily through traditional rooms, you sleep on a futon rather than a raised bed, and you leave your shoes at the entrance. I do not expect guests to know all of this before they arrive. It is something you understand quite naturally after living here for a few days.

The Kind of Japan I Like to Find When I Travel

I hope guests enjoy Japan's temples, shrines, gardens, and busy cities. I enjoy those places too.

But I also hope they notice the quieter parts of a stay: waking in a tatami room, walking through the neighbourhood, buying something at the local supermarket, and returning to an ordinary Japanese house at the end of the day.

The Showa House is in Hirakata, between Kyoto and Osaka. It can be a base for visiting Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, and Uji, but it is also a place where you can simply spend a few days living in a Japanese neighbourhood.

This is the kind of experience I look for when I travel. I wanted to make room for people who travel with the same curiosity.

Welcoming guests here has also made me look at the house differently. Things that once seemed completely ordinary to me have become interesting because someone from another part of the world noticed them.

Perhaps that is one of the nicest things about travel: meeting other people can help you see your own home again.
