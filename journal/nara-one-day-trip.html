NARA DAY TRIP · SEPTEMBER 2026

A Relaxed Day in Nara — Deer, Daibutsu and the Quiet Side of the Old Capital

Morning: the Nara everybody comes to see. Afternoon: the Nara you may remember more clearly.

Nara is an easy day trip from The Showa House and a good place to let one day have two different rhythms. In the morning, enjoy the Nara that first appears in guidebooks: deer beneath the trees of Nara Park and the immense Great Buddha at Todaiji.

Then allow the pace to change. A garden, old streets, small shops, and a temple with a quieter presence lead you into another side of the old capital. This route does not avoid famous Nara. It enjoys it fully, then leaves enough time to notice what comes afterwards.

From The Showa House

From Kuzuha Station, take the Keihan Main Line to Tambabashi. Follow the signs to the connected Kintetsu-Tambabashi Station, then continue by Kintetsu Railway to Kintetsu Nara Station.

For this itinerary, Kintetsu Nara Station is more convenient than JR Nara Station because it places you closer to Nara Park and Todaiji at the beginning of the day. Train services and connections can change, so check Google Maps or another current journey planner on the day you travel.

Morning — Nara Park

From Kintetsu Nara Station, walk toward Nara Park and let the deer be your first introduction to the city. Some may be resting beneath the trees; others will already have noticed the people selling shika senbei, the special crackers offered to the deer.

The deer are charming, but they are still animals with strong ideas about snacks. Keep maps, tickets, and other paper secure, offer the crackers carefully, and give the deer space when they need it. A polite bow does not always mean they have stopped looking for one more cracker.

Todaiji Temple and the Great Buddha

Continue through the park toward Todaiji. The approach prepares you slowly: the great gate, the heavy wooden structures, and finally the Great Buddha Hall. Even if you have seen many photographs, they do not explain the scale very well.

Step inside and look up at the Daibutsu. This is one of those places where being physically present matters. The height of the hall, the weight of the timber, and the calm expression of the Buddha are much easier to understand when they surround you.

Taka’s Note — The Pillar Challenge

Inside the Great Buddha Hall is a wooden pillar with a small square opening near its base. The opening is said to be about the same size as one of the Great Buddha’s nostrils.

It is also said that passing safely through the opening may bring good health or a step toward enlightenment. Children often slip through quite easily. Adults may discover that enlightenment has a size limit.

There is no need to force the matter. Even if you have no intention of trying it yourself, stop for a moment and watch other visitors take the challenge. The careful planning, encouragement, and final expressions are part of the experience inside the hall.

Late Morning — Yoshikien Garden

After enjoying the charm of the deer and the scale of Todaiji, slow the pace for a while at Yoshikien Garden.

Notice the moss, stones, water, and the way the garden reveals itself a little at a time. Yoshikien is not simply another stop to complete. It is the turning point between the famous Nara of the morning and the quieter Nara of the afternoon.

Lunch — Walk Toward Naramachi

From Yoshikien, begin moving south toward Naramachi. There are many possible places to eat, and I would not choose one restaurant for everyone. Look at the menus, notice which small place feels inviting, and stop when you find something that suits the day.

Choosing lunch while you walk can be more enjoyable than crossing the city for a famous name. A bowl of noodles, a set lunch, a café, or something sweet may be exactly what you need before the afternoon.

Afternoon — Naramachi

Naramachi is a place to enjoy the town itself. Old machiya houses, narrow roads, cafés, workshops, and small shops appear between ordinary homes and everyday streets. The pleasure is not in moving quickly from one landmark to another.

You do not need to take the shortest route. Turn down a street that looks interesting, stay longer in a small shop, or sit with coffee if your feet need a rest. A model course should make space for those decisions rather than remove them.

Gangoji Temple

Make Gangoji the last main destination of the afternoon. Its scale is quieter than Todaiji, but that difference is precisely why the two temples belong in the same day. Here, old roof tiles, timber, stone, and a more intimate temple ground bring the history of Nara close.

Todaiji shows you the scale of ancient Nara.
Gangoji lets you feel its age.

Give Gangoji time without asking it to compete with the Great Buddha. It offers another way to understand the old capital: not through monumentality, but through details that have endured.

Evening — Back to Nara Park

After Gangoji, wander back through Naramachi toward the centre of Nara. Stop for coffee, something sweet, or an early dinner if you find a place you like. There is no need to make the return journey a straight line.

If you still have time, walk through Nara Park once more after many day visitors have begun to leave. The paths become quieter, the light softens around the trees, and the deer settle into a different rhythm. You can experience the lively park of the morning and the calmer park of the evening within the same day.

Then return to Kintetsu Nara Station and travel back through Kintetsu-Tambabashi and Tambabashi to Kuzuha. The familiar walk from Kuzuha Station to The Showa House is a gentle final part of the day.

Two Sides of One Nara Day

The idea is not to avoid the places everybody wants to see. Meet the deer, stand beneath the Great Buddha, and enjoy the unmistakable scale of Todaiji. Then let the route become smaller and slower through Yoshikien, Naramachi, and Gangoji before returning to the park as the day grows quiet.

Afternoon: the Nara you may remember more clearly.
