Sleeping on a Japanese Futon

A small confession about tradition, comfort, and what we actually sleep on at home.

From Taka’s Notebook

Guests sometimes ask me, “Do you sleep on a futon every night?”

My answer is, “Not exactly.”

I enjoy hiking and camping, so I am used to sleeping in a sleeping bag. Sleeping on a futon for a few nights does not bother me.

At home, however, my whole family sleeps on futons with mattresses underneath. Some international guests may be sleeping on a Japanese futon for the first time, and a futon placed directly on tatami can feel harder than they expect.

That is why we use a thick, 10-centimetre mattress underneath each futon at The Showa House. I want guests to experience sleeping in a tatami room, but I also want them to sleep comfortably while traveling. I think both things can exist together.

Why Did Futons Make Sense in Japanese Homes?

When I think about traditional Japanese houses, futons seem like a very rational choice. A tatami room often did not have only one purpose.

During the day, family members could gather there, sit and talk, drink tea, or eat around a low table. At night, they took futons from the closet and laid them on the tatami. The same room became a bedroom.

In the morning, the futons were folded and stored away, and the room became living space again. Unlike a bed, which normally stays in one place, a futon can almost disappear during the day.

One room could serve several purposes depending on the time. It was an efficient use of space, and it suited the architecture and everyday life of Japanese homes at the time.

The Showa House Does It a Little Differently

The Showa House is an old Japanese house, but I do not ask guests to live exactly as people did in the past.

We keep one tatami room as the bedroom and use another tatami room as the living room. Guests do not need to fold and store their futons every morning or lay them out again every night.

After sightseeing, you can relax in the living room. When you are tired, you can simply go to the bedroom. I think this is more comfortable for travelers.

Using the rooms this way does reduce the number of people the house can accommodate. If every tatami room were used for sleeping, more guests could stay. But I deliberately prefer fewer guests with more comfortable living space: a room for sleeping, and a room for relaxing.

Keeping the Japanese Feeling Without Recreating the Past Exactly

I do not want The Showa House to be a museum or an exact recreation of life decades ago. I want guests to experience the smell and feel of tatami, sleeping on a futon, waking in a Japanese room, and seeing morning light through the shoji.

But I do not think you need to live exactly as people did in the past in order to experience Japan. The traditional way was rational for the homes and lifestyles of its time. The Showa House simply adapts that tradition for people traveling today.

That is why the futon has a thick, 10-centimetre mattress underneath, the bedroom can remain a bedroom during your stay, and there is a separate tatami living room. I want you to enjoy the Japanese feeling, but I do not think you need to live exactly as people did in the past.

If You Want to Fold Your Futon Every Morning...

Of course, if you say, “I came to Japan, so I want to fold my futon every morning, put it away, and lay it out again at night,” I will happily respect that choice. If you want to try it, I will not stop you.

It can actually be an interesting experience. Once the futon is stored away, the bedroom becomes an ordinary tatami room again. Laying it out at night turns the room back into a bedroom. It gives you a small sense of how Japanese families traditionally used one room differently throughout the day.

But this is certainly not required at The Showa House. If you come home tired after sightseeing, you can leave the futon exactly where it is and simply go to sleep.

Old Japan, Just a Little More Comfortable

I do not believe preserving an old house means everything must remain exactly as it was decades ago. Traditional Japanese life had its own logic and efficiency. Folding away a futon and using one room for several purposes is a very clever system.

Modern travelers have different needs, and The Showa House tries to respect both. You still sleep in a tatami room, use a futon, and experience a traditional Japanese house. But there is a thick mattress underneath the futon, the bedroom can remain a bedroom, and you have a separate tatami living room in which to relax.

Old Japan, just a little more comfortable.

Because, in the end, I want you to remember Japan—not a sore back.
